DWG P-01 · Wearable Robotics

Real-Time Actuator Control for a Hip Exosuit

A tethered MATLAB/Simulink controller for the CubeMars AK70-9 actuator that drives an OpenExo-based hip exosuit, built in TMU's SymBioRobotX lab for occupational load-carriage assistance.

The problem

The lab moved exosuit control off embedded firmware and onto MATLAB/Simulink on a lab PC so control strategies could be iterated quickly. That meant bringing up a quasi-direct-drive motor over raw CAN, with timing good enough to eventually drive gait-phase assistance, and no safety net: the driver simply holds its last command if the bus drops.

What I did

  • Simulink Desktop Real-Time controller with a four-position mode selector (position / velocity / torque / disable), decoded feedback frames, and a scope that follows the active mode, running over PCAN-USB at 1 Mbps.
  • Moved it to real-time kernel mode, which eliminated a ~4 s feedback lag seen in normal mode.
  • Debugged silent CAN faults by hand-decoding frames in PCAN-View: a copy-pasted ×10,000 gain on the velocity channel was being clamped by the driver with no error code, and current commands were going to the current-brake ID instead of the current loop (the ID fault was found in a debugging session with my supervisor).
  • Tuned the position loop in four runs, changing one driver gain (Kp 0.0040 → 0.0170). Closed-loop bandwidth rose 4.9×, from 1.07 Hz to 5.23 Hz, and a 5 Hz sine returned 72% of commanded amplitude against the 70.7% (−3 dB) acceptance line. Bandwidth scaled linearly with Kp across all four points, so the next target is one parameter write away.
  • Checked the tuning against a step response: 61 ms rise time (67 ms predicted), no overshoot above the 0.1° feedback resolution, and a steady-state error of 0.001°, so derivative and integral gains stayed untouched.
  • Confirmed the current-command scaling on the bench (mA on the V3.0 frame), which cleared up an apparent 10× disagreement between two torque-constant references.
  • Helped redesign and assemble the exosuit's mechanical architecture on the OpenExo platform, from the 3D-printed motor cartridges and strain-relief parts to the sprocket, roller-chain, and Bowden-cable transmission that carries motor torque to the joint.
  • Designed the 12-position CAN / 48 V tether (Amphenol AT-series, Rev D) with a pin map, linear-bus termination, and a mandatory pre-connection continuity and isolation test, built from in-stock parts instead of 10–14-week-lead M23 connectors.
  • Wrote an 8-block bench test plan (identity → passive → open-loop → position → MIT → torque → thermal → fault/stop) and designed a 3D-printed motor testbed with a 100–250 mm lever arm, sized around torque reaction rather than motor weight.

Result

Position bandwidth 1.07 → 5.23 Hz · ~4 s lag → real-time feedback · torque control running

Next

Raising the control loop from 100 Hz to 200 Hz, measuring real output torque with the lever-arm testbed and a known mass (the datasheet torque constant has not yet been checked against the output shaft), and adding an external controller with feedforward to cut the remaining ~30 ms of position-tracking lag.

DWG P-04 · Microfabrication

Mother Machine: Microfluidics for Single-Cell Imaging

An end-to-end fabrication process for a mother-machine microfluidic device, built at iBEST's Miniaturization in Bioanalysis Lab so future students can image single E. coli cells over many generations.

The problem

A mother machine traps bacteria single-file in dead-end growth channels about one cell wide, with fresh media flowing past the open ends. The cell at the closed end, the "mother", can be tracked across many generations, which is how you would study questions like why dividing E. coli are more vulnerable to the antimicrobial peptide LL-37. The catch: those channels are about 1.3 µm wide and 1.2 µm tall, and the process has to be reproducible by whoever comes next.

What I did

  • Two-layer SU-8 photolithography on 100 mm silicon wafers with a MicroWriter ML3 maskless direct-write system: ~1.2 µm growth channels from a TF 6001 / TF 6005 blend, and a main trench from SU-8 2010 confirmed at ~18 µm by optical profilometry.
  • Calibration first: iterated test photomasks across exposure doses before committing to the device, then aligned Layer 2 to Layer 1 with vernier scales and a spot-development step over the fiducials, which was validated and promoted into the main procedure.
  • Finished masters with a ramped hard bake before FOTCS vapour silanization, then cast PDMS replicas, punched ports, and plasma bonded devices.
  • Diagnosed a Layer 1 thickness discrepancy: profilometry read roughly half the target and didn't change with a resist blend that should have thickened the film, which points to a thin-film optical-model artifact rather than a coating failure. Designed two discriminating experiments: re-measuring a metal-coated coupon, and loading E. coli as a biological ruler.
  • Authored the lab's 30-section cleanroom SOP (v3.0), wrote a Python GDS viewer for mask files, and designed a 3D-printed microscope stage insert in SolidWorks.

Result

Reproducible two-layer process and SOP v3.0, handed off Aug 2026

Open at handoff

The Layer 1 thickness question was still unresolved when the contract ended; the two discriminating experiments were left as the next student's first steps rather than completed.

P-07Biosignals

EMG Signal Pipeline

MATLAB pipeline that turns raw muscle signals into %MVC activation.

DWG P-07 · Biosignals

EMG Signal Processing Pipeline

A MATLAB pipeline that turns raw surface EMG from the tibialis anterior into a normalized activation curve.

The problem

Raw EMG is noisy and in arbitrary units. It picks up motion artifact at the low end and 60 Hz mains hum, and its amplitude changes between people and electrode placements. It has to be cleaned and normalized before it says anything about effort.

What I did

  • Band-pass filtered 20–450 Hz (4th order) with an optional 60 Hz notch, on signals sampled at 2 kHz.
  • Full-wave rectified the signal and extracted a 100 ms RMS envelope.
  • Normalized to %MVC against the peak envelope of a maximum-voluntary-contraction trial, so a ramped-effort trial reads as a percentage of the person's own maximum.
  • Checked the filtering in the frequency domain with a raw-vs-filtered power spectral density.

Result

Clean, comparable activation curves in %MVC
